180 days of health promises.One missing word:measles.

The government’s six-month health scorecard lists repairs, recruitment steps and plans for reform. It does not mention measles.

This report follows the distance between those promises and the care people received: an outbreak that kept sending children to hospital, families paying for treatment, and a health system short of the staff and services they needed.

The Health Services Division tracked 12 commitments during the BNP government’s first 180 days. Its scorecard records repairs, proposals, recruitment steps and legislation; it does not mention measles. Reading it alongside the reported outbreak reveals the distance between administrative progress and care that reaches people. First, the promises that set the direction.

What the government promised

The manifesto’s 22 healthcare proposals promised a different experience of public care: more workers, a digital record, treatment closer to home and a gradual rise in spending. These six commitments set the direction. They are promises for a five-year term; the government’s own 180-day targets show where it chose to begin.

These commitments link money and staffing to a practical promise: treatment should be easier to reach and better organised. A five-year programme will not be completed in six months. But the first 180 days can show whether the foundations are being laid. The minister’s dated statements set out his priorities; the scorecard that follows records the government’s chosen milestones.

The next chapter follows a health emergency that continued while those services were still being built.

Measles, beyond day 180

The scorecard ended.
The outbreak did not.

For a family beside a hospital bed, the test of a health system is immediate: can their child get care? By the time the government closed its 180-day scorecard, reported measles and measles-like deaths had reached 902. By 9 September, the toll was 1,002. Behind that rising count were children needing treatment and families waiting with them.

A campaign is only as strong as its reach.

A national total can make a vaccination campaign look complete. The next comparison asks who was reached, and where the gaps remained. Those differences matter to the children preventive care is meant to protect.

Before this government

A gap carried forward

Routine immunisation had been disrupted since 2020. Vitamin-A and deworming campaigns lapsed under the interim administration, leaving preventive services behind schedule.

During the response

A campaign that missed children

The government launched vaccination rapidly. The minister later described the campaign target as inaccurate. Reporting also identified weak local planning and vaccine hesitancy.

“This is criticism for the sake of criticism. We have not neglected any aspect of disease management. Treatment protocols were followed, and beds were provided.”

Sardar Md Sakhawat HusainHealth Minister · 10 Sep 2026, by phone from a WHO event abroad

The monthly decline did not hold: cases and deaths rose again in August. The two campaigns also reached different numbers of children in the same age group, with the largest proportional gap in Mymensingh. Together, the figures show why a campaign total alone cannot describe protection across the country. For families who reached hospital, the consequences included another burden: the cost of care.

What treatment cost families

Families still paid out of pocket for a child’s treatment in government hospitals. Reported costs ranged from Tk 16,000 to Tk 65,000. Among surveyed families, 78% worked in the informal sector, with monthly incomes of Tk 6,000-20,000. The financial burden lasted beyond the hospital stay.

Borrowing was the most common response among the 2,746 households surveyed, and many also used savings. These are overlapping ways of paying the same bill. Free treatment did not remove the wider expense of seeking care. The next part looks beyond the outbreak, at the other health pressures that compete for the same limited services.

The pressures beyond measles

Measles was the most visible emergency, but the demands on the health system extend further. Dengue prevention, malaria elimination, family planning and long-term treatment each depend on services that keep working between outbreaks.

These charts describe different timelines, but each points to the need for sustained services. Dengue prevention needs surveillance; malaria’s remaining burden is concentrated geographically; family planning depends on reliable supplies. Cancer and kidney care add continuing treatment needs. Meeting those demands requires both resources and the ability to turn them into working services.

The gap between funding and delivery

The FY2026-27 health budget rose to Tk 69,409 crore, the largest allocation yet. At 1.01% of GDP, it remained well below the manifesto’s gradual 5% goal. The spending record adds another question: how effectively can allocations become services?

A larger allocation is a real change, yet the gap to the 5% pledge remains substantial. Low development spending in the reported ten-month period shows that allocation and delivery are separate hurdles. The evidence points to both a funding gap and an implementation gap. That is the context for the final disagreement: how much time reform needs, and what people should already be able to receive.

How the first six months are judged

The government describes the first six months as the start of a five-year programme. The experts quoted here judge the same period by progress on the essentials of care. Their differing views bring the story back to what the first 180 days actually delivered.

The record contains completed actions, plans still in preparation and longstanding failures that cross administrations. The central question is whether progress on paper becomes staffed facilities, reliable supplies and treatment within reach. By day 180, the measles toll was 902. By 9 September it was 1,002. The reporting window closed on a health system still facing that test.
